About

I am a PhD student in Computer Science at the University of Genova, working on runtime verification and industrial automation with a focus on programmable logic controllers (PLCs) and safe control software. My background combines academic research with software engineering: before the doctorate I worked on large-scale data and security systems (Apache Spark, Kafka, and related stacks) and delivered full-stack products in industry and freelance settings.

I am interested in formal methods, dependable systems, and how rigorous specification and monitoring can improve safety and reliability in real plants and products. I also use modern AI-assisted tooling to accelerate research and implementation workflows.

I completed visiting doctoral activity at the University of Malta (December 2025 – February 2026). Outside work I enjoy building useful software, open-source tooling, and continuous learning.

Research

My doctoral work centers on making runtime verification practical for PLC-based systems: specifying expected behaviour, monitoring execution, and connecting to industrial toolchains—together with experimentation and tooling such as transpilation from Structured Text (ST) to C where it helps integration and analysis.

Runtime verification for PLCs

Designing and evaluating an RV framework for programmable logic controllers, with attention to safe program organization units and meaningful experimental validation in realistic settings.

Specification languages & tooling

Using C as a specification language where it fits existing workflows; building bridges such as an ST-to-C transpiler for cross-platform compatibility and performance.

Formal rewards & reinforcement learning

RMLGym: a formal reward-machine-oriented angle on reinforcement learning—linking formal property-based structure with RL settings (see publications below).

From data pipelines to dependable software

Prior experience includes high-throughput streaming analytics and full-stack delivery; I care about correctness, observability, and maintainability—not only in research prototypes but in production-grade systems.
